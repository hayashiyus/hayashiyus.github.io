The first clue was 85 unfinished answers.

When Astra asked Qwen3.5-0.8B to solve 96 practice problems, eleven answers were correct. The other 85 ran through the 32,768-token budget without reaching an answer. Their final passages repeated the same phrases over and over. Before any geometric analysis, Astra already had a useful lead: a recognizable pattern of failure.3

The eventual repair changed selected columns in two MLP matrices. Takehi and colleagues reported that MATH-500 accuracy rose from 7.9% to 48.1%, while the fraction of answers hitting the token limit fell from 93.0% to 33.1%. Gradient-based training was not allowed. We distinguish those reported results from our own experiments, which examine how the edit works inside the model and how far local geometry can explain its effects.1

How did an LLM find where—and in which direction—to intervene among hundreds of millions of parameters?

A trained network has places where internal features can be measured and their effects changed. We can observe activations, modify the matrices that write them into the residual stream, and check what happens to the output. Astra used this structure to find a compact edit, with a different direction at each edited layer. During inference, that edit acts as activation steering whose strength depends on the current state.

Information geometry gives us a way to measure the effect of such an edit. It also describes which steering interventions are possible when every input must use the same weights. These ideas yield predictions we can test. They also have limits: at the full strength of Astra's edit, the local prediction breaks down.

Astra did not need to understand the meaning of every weight. It found a measurable failure and a linear projection where a short rule could act on it. The edit reads a scalar from the incoming activations and adds a correction along a fixed direction. This raises two questions: can one shared matrix produce the corrections we want, and how much do those corrections change the output distribution? The first concerns a reachable subspace; the second concerns a metric. This structure lets us reason about the search without equating parameter count with the size of an unstructured search space.

We will follow the failure Astra observed, the program it wrote into the weights, and the geometry that describes how that program acts.

What the evidence covers. The large benchmark improvement comes from the original authors. Our experiments comprise a reconstruction on 40 prefixes, local-KL measurements on 32 prefixes, and a steering-control experiment using artificially repetitive text. A prefix is the sequence of preceding tokens supplied to the model. A small generation pilot is complete; full MATH-500 reproduction and the fixed-program search study remain unfinished. The paintings are allegories. The mathematical surfaces come from explicitly defined models.

1. What could Astra observe?

Start with the task and the tools. The initial prompt gave Astra Qwen's architecture, functions for reading and writing weights, labeled practice problems, and tools for collecting outputs and activations. It even named repetition as an example of a failure worth repairing. Astra was asked to classify failures, test interventions rather than stop at correlations, vary their strength, and check the results on unused questions.2

The run with a budget of 50 evaluation-tool calls could repeat this cycle of observation and intervention. Here, “50” counts tool calls, not model forward passes: one call could run hundreds of generations or evaluate supplied text under teacher forcing. Astra inspected answers, measured internal quantities, tried edits, and used behavior on held-out questions to decide what to try next.

The logs preserve failed requests and inconclusive early results. A single-neuron ablation and a subsequent removal of a direction in one layer each produced a noisy +6.25 percentage-point change on 32 problems. A later edit to layers 15 and 19 together produced a much larger held-out improvement. The repair emerged through this sequence of trials.

Three features of the setup made the search tractable. First, the failure was visible: many answers followed the same pattern of prolonged repetition ending at the token limit. Second, access to activations and log-probabilities made it possible to compare repetitive and non-repetitive text quantitatively. Third, the MLP down-projection provided a linear path for writing into the residual stream. This structure made a short corrective program possible. The results alone do not tell us how much of the success came from Astra's reasoning, the prompt, the tools, or a failure that happened to be amenable to this kind of repair.

The shorter 10-call and 25-call runs matter too. Both identified broadly the same failure, but their edits were less effective. The 10-call run applied a weaker rank-one-style operation to many output matrices; the 25-call run disabled an attention head. Reaching the same diagnosis did not guarantee finding the same solution. An account of the success must also explain why these nearby attempts achieved less.

The public logs report three contrasting runs. All three are the original authors' results, not our reruns.

It would also be premature to conclude that the edit gave Qwen new mathematical abilities. Although the evaluation used mathematics problems, the observed bottleneck was finishing an answer. On the original confirmation set, the edited model completed many answers that had previously exhausted the token budget. Correct answers after editing were longer, rather than merely ending sooner. That pattern is consistent with removing repetition that prevented existing competence from producing an answer. It does not establish that a low-rank intervention added general arithmetic or algebraic ability.

Evaluation should preserve that distinction. Alongside overall accuracy, we should count answers rescued by the edit, previously correct answers lost, completed but incorrect answers, answer lengths, and degeneration into repeated whitespace.

The practical lesson is to make the failure measurable before choosing an edit family. An aggregate score cannot tell us whether errors share an internal state, reflect a missing feature, arise from decoding, or have unrelated causes. The 85/96 observation narrowed the search before a geometric interpretation was needed.

2. How does a 0.016% edit act as a program?

The figure uses a precisely defined toy model to compare two operations. It is not a recording of Qwen activations. Take three states, A=(1,0), B=(0,1), and C=(-1,0), with W=I, k=(1,0), and d=(0,.7). Adding ΔW=d kᵀ to the matrix and adding d(kᵀa) through an activation hook produce the same local change for each input: +d for A, zero for B, and −d for C.

An external intervention applied only once to A does not, by itself, define what should happen to later states. The animation illustrates this distinction and the identity behind it. It is not evidence that the model's naturally occurring loops have been detected.

The actual edit acts on the MLP down-projections in layers 15 and 19. Let WWW map MLP-neuron activations a(x)a(x)a(x) into the residual stream. The mechanism follows from an exact identity: ΔW=dk⊤⟹Δh(x)=d [k⊤a(x)].\boxed{\Delta W=d k^\top \quad\Longrightarrow\quad \Delta h(x)=d\,[k^\top a(x)].}ΔW=dk⊤⟹Δh(x)=d[k⊤a(x)].​ Read a scalar from the current state, multiply a direction vector by it, and add the result to the activations. The scalar varies continuously and can be positive or negative. Calling it a “gate” does not make it a binary loop detector. Astra's idealized edit at each layer takes the form

ΔW=−g u (m⊙u⊤W),\Delta W=-g\,u\,(m\odot u^\top W),ΔW=−gu(m⊙u⊤W),

where uuu is a unit direction, mmm is a mask selecting columns, and ggg is the gain controlling intervention strength. The resulting activation change is

Δh(x)=ΔWa(x)=−g u [m⊙(u⊤W)]a(x).\Delta h(x)=\Delta W a(x)=-g\,u\,[m\odot(u^\top W)]a(x).Δh(x)=ΔWa(x)=−gu[m⊙(u⊤W)]a(x).

The vector uuu determines which direction to move. The scalar multiplying it depends on the current MLP activations and determines when and how strongly to move. The weights therefore store a state-dependent steering rule, rather than an instruction to add the same vector every time.

Think of a river and a rudder. Editing the weights changes a shared rule for how the current state is acted on. Direct steering changes the action applied at a particular moment. Both can respond to the current state—a rudder can be adjusted to the flow—but a shared weight edit must apply the same rule to every input. The river is an analogy for that rule, not a literal picture of the network's dynamics.

At a linear projection, the possible interventions can be organized as follows:

  • Add a constant vector, also achievable with an editable bias: Δh=d\Delta h=dΔh=d.
  • Read one scalar and use it to scale a direction: Δh=d[k⊤a(x)]\Delta h=d[k^\top a(x)]Δh=d[k⊤a(x)].
  • Assign an output vector to each neuron: Δh=ΔWa(x)\Delta h=\Delta W a(x)Δh=ΔWa(x).
  • Specify an arbitrary change for each input: Δh=v(x)\Delta h=v(x)Δh=v(x).

This is a classification of interventions at a linear interface, not a theorem about minimizing KL. A bias-free matrix, for example, cannot necessarily add the same nonzero shift to every possible input; an editable bias makes that operation available. Nor can a single shared matrix generally implement an arbitrary field of changes. Its Jacobian determines which changes are reachable.

In the actual edit, each of the two layers has its own direction and 64 selected columns. The published formula remains rank one after masking, before rounding to bfloat16. The saved weight difference also contains small rounding residuals. The original evaluation used those saved weights; the ideal formula explains the mechanism. Keeping the two distinct matters for reconstruction.

In E1, we compared the saved weight edit with its additive activation intervention on fixed prefixes. Across 40 prefixes in FP32, the maximum logit difference was 2.0×10−52.0\times10^{-5}2.0×10−5, and the maximum KL was 9.6×10−129.6\times10^{-12}9.6×10−12. Differences were larger in bfloat16: a maximum logit difference of 0.3047 and maximum KL of 0.00713. This experiment checks numerical reconstruction of the edit. It does not provide a new task-performance result.

The gain matters to the interpretation. A gain of one removes the selected columns' component along uuu. A gain of six changes that component to approximately negative five times its original value, reversing it rather than merely removing it. Where the selected neurons write along the contrast direction, the edit adds a contribution in the opposite direction. Calling this only an “ablation” obscures the feedback-like behavior. It still does not establish that the gate selectively detects every naturally occurring loop.

With edits at multiple layers, the activations used by each intervention matter too. Layer 19 receives inputs already affected by the edit at layer 15. The additive identity remains exact when a(x)a(x)a(x) comes from the edited execution. Replaying gate values recorded from the unedited model does not exactly reproduce the edited model. The switch must be evaluated from the activations that arrive at that moment.

3. Did the switch need to depend on the state?

To test whether the state-dependent switch matters, replace it with a constant intervention along uuu. If the two have the same effect on repetitive text, which better preserves text associated with useful output? The preregistered E2 comparison included Astra's original gate, a constant intervention, shuffled gate values, and a random control. Two limitations constrain what the measurements can tell us.

First, the repetition was constructed. The E2 manifest explicitly describes a test using synthetic continuations under teacher forcing, rather than repetition generated by the model. It uses 16 calibration cases and 32 evaluation cases. Repetitive text is made by taking a reference-answer prefix and repeating its final 16-token block. Qwen did not enter these loops during generation. The experiment probes the edit's response to a controlled state; it does not directly reproduce the failure Astra observed.

Second, the effects were not successfully matched. The constant, shuffled, and random conditions all failed the predeclared effect-matching check. Their calibration residuals relative to the target were +0.1312, −0.0266, and +0.1701, respectively. With effects unmatched on the tested gain grid, we cannot make a clean comparison of side effects at equal benefit.

The results are still informative about response patterns. The original gate responds differently from the controls, and we can compare responses at teacher-forced positions in productive and repetitive sequences. But this does not show that thermostat-like, state-dependent control is necessary during natural generation.

All three calibration curves bracket the target, so a finer gain search may improve the match. The next experiment should keep the same controls, refine that search, and construct contrast pairs from answers the model actually generates on practice problems. Calibration and evaluation data must remain separate, and matching the target effect must be a prerequisite for comparison. We can then ask how the controls change the probability of entering or escaping a loop during generation, beyond their effect on the log-probability of a synthetic continuation.

4. How far can Fisher geometry predict the effect?

A norm measured directly in weight coordinates can be a poor guide to an edit's effect. Two weight changes of the same size can change the output distribution by very different amounts. For a fixed prefix and a small activation displacement vvv, the next-token KL has the local expansion

KL(ph∥ph+v)=12v⊤Fhv+o(∥v∥2),\mathrm{KL}(p_h\Vert p_{h+v}) =\tfrac12 v^\top F_hv+o(\|v\|^2),KL(ph​∥ph+v​)=21​v⊤Fh​v+o(∥v∥2),

where FhF_hFh​ is the output Fisher matrix pulled back to activation coordinates. This metric measures how distinguishable the distributions are. It does not measure whether an answer is correct or its meaning is preserved. A low-KL intervention can still damage an unmeasured capability. Keeping KL low on text associated with useful output is a design objective we choose, not a guarantee supplied by Fisher geometry.

E3 tested this prediction on 32 evaluation prefixes. We estimated Fisher separately for each prefix using finite differences, and fitted the scalar coefficients of the norm baselines on 16 separate calibration prefixes. The evaluation strengths—0.03, 0.1, 0.3, and 1.0—were excluded from the local fit.

At strength 0.03, the median absolute prediction error on a log scale was 0.013 decades for Fisher, compared with 0.479 for the weight-norm baseline. At strength 1, the errors were 0.462 and 0.420, respectively. By this measure, Fisher was substantially more accurate for the small intervention, but slightly worse at the original edit strength.

At full strength, the median ratio of the Fisher prediction to measured KL, computed separately for each prefix, was about 0.38. This is the median of those ratios, not the ratio of the two medians. Agreement at the small strengths used to estimate or check derivatives cannot be counted as independent evidence of predictive accuracy.

Strength 1 corresponds to Astra's gain-six edit. At the strength that improved the original task, the local Fisher approximation was no longer accurate. Task improvement therefore does not validate the local approximation: an edit can leave the local regime and still help. The metric describes nearby changes; it does not automatically describe an entire generation.

These measurements also condition on a fixed prefix. During generation, changing the next token changes subsequent prefixes and their Fisher matrices. A theory of the whole sequence would need to specify both the sequence distribution and the intervention path. The fixed-prefix result cannot simply be extended to the entire rollout. Our theory note keeps these questions separate and leaves guarantees over finite trajectories as an open extension.

Conditions of the local-KL experiment

We computed KL in FP32 using ordinary softmax distributions at fixed prefixes. The calculation excludes the top-k and top-p truncation used during generation. The primary finite-difference step was 0.001; steps of 0.003 and 0.01 were used to check the derivative estimate. Only one saved edit direction was tested.

The activation-norm baseline uses activations from the intervened execution, so it is not a prediction available from the unedited state alone. This experiment also does not establish that a Fisher estimate transfers to another direction or another prefix.

We can now make the duality precise. There are two related constructions here, and they need to be distinguished.

The first is an adjoint and metric relationship between weight edits and activation fields. Let EEE map the permitted weight coordinates θ\thetaθ to the state at the intervention interface, with derivative J=DEJ=DEJ=DE. This state may collect activations across many inputs. With the downstream computation held fixed, it must capture every effect of the weight edit on that computation. This is the requirement for a complete intervention interface.

Pulling the activation-space metric FFF back through JJJ gives the weight-space metric Gθ=J⊤FJG_\theta=J^\top FJGθ​=J⊤FJ.

To specify which metric defines the gradient, we use the metric-gradient operator ∇M\nabla_M∇M​. It converts the differential of a scalar objective into a direction using the metric MMM. For positive-definite MMM and every tangent vector vvv, it satisfies ⟨∇Mf,v⟩M=df[v]⟺∇Mf=M−1∂f.\langle\nabla_M f,v\rangle_M=df[v] \quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad \nabla_M f=M^{-1}\partial f.⟨∇M​f,v⟩M​=df[v]⟺∇M​f=M−1∂f. Here df[v]df[v]df[v] is the first-order change in fff along vvv, and ∂f\partial f∂f collects the ordinary coordinate derivatives in a column. Setting M=IM=IM=I recovers the Euclidean gradient; using a Fisher metric gives the natural gradient. The subscript specifies the metric, not the direction of differentiation. This operation raises the differential's index with the inverse metric. It is distinct from taking the covariant derivative of a vector field.

In identifiable coordinates, or on the quotient by null directions described below, a smooth objective CCC satisfies J ∇Gθ(C∘E)=Πim⁡JF ∇FC.\boxed{J\,\nabla_{G_\theta}(C\circ E) =\Pi_{\operatorname{im}J}^{F}\,\nabla_{F}C.}J∇Gθ​​(C∘E)=ΠimJF​∇F​C.​ The left-hand side is the activation change induced by moving the weights along their natural gradient. The right-hand side projects the activation-space natural gradient onto the changes those weights can realize, using the Fisher metric. Locally, taking a natural-gradient step in weight space and mapping it to activations gives the same result as taking the natural gradient in activation space and projecting it onto the reachable directions.

For a single linear write projection, Jx=a(x)⊤⊗IJ_x=a(x)^\top\otimes IJx​=a(x)⊤⊗I, and averaging the pullback metric over inputs gives E[(aa⊤)⊗Fx]\mathbb E[(aa^\top)\otimes F_x]E[(aa⊤)⊗Fx​]. If the Fisher metric itself is singular, work on an identifiable quotient space and require the objective differential to vanish on null directions. Simply substituting a Euclidean pseudoinverse does not make the statement coordinate-invariant.

The downstream map must remain fixed. For interventions at multiple sites, account for their dependencies through a complete interface or an explicit intervention graph. The identity establishes local agreement; it does not imply that optimization trajectories coincide after finite steps.

The second is Amari's duality between natural and expectation coordinates, which appears when the output is represented by an exponential family. If hhh denotes softmax natural coordinates and η=∇ψ(h)\eta=\nabla\psi(h)η=∇ψ(h) the expected sufficient statistics, then

η˙=q⟺h˙=F(h)−1q.\dot\eta=q \quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad \dot h=F(h)^{-1}q.η˙​=q⟺h˙=F(h)−1q.

A straight path in expectation coordinates generally becomes a curve in natural coordinates, and vice versa. This provides a mathematical basis for “dual steering” at the output representation. It does not make the weights themselves expectation coordinates, or make every hidden layer dually flat. Nonlinear intermediate computation, rank deficiency, and unidentifiable directions each require their own conditions.

Together, these relations give us a mathematical account of the Qwen edit: a rank-one update can apply a direction with input-dependent strength, and a Fisher-weighted alternative could reduce output KL while preserving a fixed local target effect. They do not explain how Astra chose its contrast examples, why the two layers looked promising, or why it tried gain six. The logs show a search that adapted to observations, not evidence that these choices came from a Fisher calculation.

It is important here to distinguish a vector from a covector. An activation difference between two classes of text is a displacement vector: it describes how the representation changes. The differential of a behavioral objective is a covector: it measures how much that objective changes under a small displacement. Using the Fisher metric to relate them requires specifying the metric and a local output model.

Replacing every contrast vector with F−1vF^{-1}vF−1v does not automatically produce natural-gradient steering; it confuses two different mathematical objects. The proposed E5 experiment specifies the objective, the positions at which cost is measured, damping, and the effect-matching procedure in advance. Its candidate direction is defined under those conditions, rather than by a general prescription to precondition every useful direction.

The phrase “compiled into weights” carries the same constraints. It means that an editable matrix implements a particular field of activation changes across a set of inputs. The resulting program need not be independent of those inputs, cannot generally implement arbitrary fields, and is not guaranteed to preserve every other function. A shared matrix links its effects across contexts. Even a successful edit is one choice from a constrained family of functions, and its side effects are part of what that program does.

6. Why do shared weights make some steering requests impossible?

Consider a model with just one weight, hw(x)=wxh_w(x)=wxhw​(x)=wx, evaluated at x=1x=1x=1 and x=−1x=-1x=−1. Changing the weight by δw\delta wδw shifts the two activations by +δw+\delta w+δw and −δw-\delta w−δw. The shifts necessarily point in opposite directions.

Now suppose we want the same positive shift ccc for both inputs. Direct activation steering can add (c,c)(c,c)(c,c). A shared weight edit would have to satisfy both δw=c\delta w=cδw=c and −δw=c-\delta w=c−δw=c, which is impossible for c≠0c\ne0c=0.

This small example exposes the constraint. A weight edit cannot specify an independent intervention for every input: the same parameter change links all of its effects. More generally, consider the local linearization, in which JJJ maps permitted weight displacements to activation displacements. Within this linearized model, only changes in im⁡J\operatorname{im}JimJ are reachable. For a reachable change uuu, with a positive-definite metric MMM measuring resource cost in weight space, the minimum cost is

min⁡Jv=u12v⊤Mv=12u⊤(JM−1J⊤)+u.\min_{Jv=u}\tfrac12v^\top Mv =\tfrac12u^\top(JM^{-1}J^\top)^+u.Jv=umin​21​v⊤Mv=21​u⊤(JM−1J⊤)+u.

If the requested change lies outside im⁡J\operatorname{im}JimJ, no edit satisfies the constraint, and the cost is +∞+\infty+∞. Evaluating the pseudoinverse expression alone must not be used to assign a finite cost to an unreachable request. These feasibility and cost statements are exact for a linear interface. For a nonlinear model, an infeasible linearized request does not by itself rule out a finite edit. Dual variables can certify infeasibility in the linearized model. Such a certificate can guide a practical decision: add a bias, edit another layer, relax a constraint on a protected context, or stop searching.

Fisher alone also cannot select a sparse edit. Different weight changes can induce the same local activation field and therefore have the same output Fisher cost. Choosing 64 columns, two layers, or a particular sparse mask requires an additional resource constraint, architectural prior, or search rule. Astra's mask was observed as part of a successful program; the metric does not determine it uniquely.

8. What have we learned, and what remains open?

The strongest supported conclusion about this Qwen repair is that Astra found a compact weight-space program that acts as state-dependent activation steering. The public logs record the observed failure, contrast measurements, causal interventions, gain sweeps, and improvements on questions not used in the search.

Our E1 experiment reconstructed the edit as an additive intervention and confirmed numerical agreement in FP32. E3 found that Fisher geometry predicted small KL changes at fixed prefixes well, but the prediction broke down at the original gain-six strength. E2 measured responses to constructed sequences. Because those sequences did not arise from natural generation and the control effects were not matched, it cannot establish whether the switch is necessary during generation.

Information geometry offers three ideas for designing edits. First, output KL defines a local cost whose quadratic approximation predicts an edit's effect; our experiment identifies a regime where it outperforms a norm baseline. Second, the permitted weight changes determine which activation fields are reachable and how effects across contexts are linked. Third, natural and expectation coordinates give a mathematical account of the relationship between a directly specified steering direction and a direction that accounts for the metric. These ideas can guide experiments and help identify impossible requests.

Several questions remain unresolved. There is no evidence that Astra used Fisher geometry. Nor have we shown that gated rank-one instructions are generally easy to discover. We have not established whether the edit added an ability or removed an obstacle to an ability the model already had. One successful edit to one failure mode in one model also cannot establish how WSP scales in general.

The case does suggest a way to study model editing by agents. Record what the agent observes, which attempts fail, and how its hypotheses change—not just the final edit. Check numerical reconstruction separately from reproducing task behavior. When adding a theoretical explanation after the fact, specify what it predicts before inspecting independent evaluation data.

Negative controls should preserve intervention magnitude while disrupting the proposed mechanism. Shuffle the gate values, use an orthogonal direction, substitute a constant intervention, or test inputs whose requirements cannot be satisfied by shared weights. These comparisons reduce the room for alternative explanations of a successful result.

The tools can be organized around those roles. First, identify a recurring or otherwise structured failure. Relate the difference in behavior to measurable differences in internal representations. Check whether the permitted weights can meet the requests across contexts, estimate output-side effects, then validate through natural generation and held-out tasks. Astra's run is one successful example of this sequence. Geometry supplies a candidate framework for the reachability and cost steps; it does not replace diagnosis or validation.

The next step is a preregistered comparison with the original behavioral target held fixed. It should use naturally generated trajectories as well as synthetic controls, keep discovery data separate from evaluation data, and test whether a direction informed by the metric predicts fewer side effects before committing to expensive generation runs. If it succeeds, geometry becomes a tool for designing edits. If it fails, the public logs still demonstrate something useful: an LLM can write a program into a large network when it can observe a failure, measure internal contrasts, access a suitable write path, and iterate within a sufficient budget.

These connections build on substantial prior work. Amari's natural gradient is established theory. Steer2Edit converts steering vectors into rank-one edits to individual model components, and FishBack directly studies a pulled-back Fisher metric for activation steering. This essay examines a particular WSP case and tests the scope and limits of its explanation. It does not claim that the underlying dualities are new.

A trained model can contain small programmable interfaces within a vast learned computation. The research questions are when we can find those interfaces, which instructions they can express, and how far a local metric can guide us before we have to rely on measurement.
